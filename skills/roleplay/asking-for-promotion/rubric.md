# Rubric: Asking for a Promotion

Calibrated for exceptionalism. Score 5 = Teresa says "I want to advocate for this." Most people score 2-3 in their first session.

---

## Scoring Dimensions

### 1. Evidence of Next-Level Operation
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the user provides specific examples of already operating at the next level — not working harder at the current level, but doing qualitatively different work.
**Things to Look For:** Are the examples about scope expansion, not just effort? Do they describe situations where the user owned something that was previously owned at a higher level?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Evidence is entirely about performing current-level responsibilities well — no qualitative difference from the current role.
**Score 2:** One example touches on next-level territory but is described in terms of work done rather than scope owned.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** One or two specific examples describe genuinely next-level scope — cross-team decisions, leading without authority, owning outcomes previously above the user's level.
**Score 4:** Three or more specific examples, each describing a situation where the user's behavior was qualitatively different from their current-level peers.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Examples are so precisely matched to the next-level role's expectations that Teresa can use them verbatim in the calibration meeting. Each example describes a specific outcome, scope, and stakeholder impact.

---

### 2. Understanding of What the Next Level Requires
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the user can articulate what the next level specifically requires — not a generic description, but the qualitative differences in scope, ownership, and judgment expected at the next level.
**Things to Look For:** Can the user name specific capabilities, responsibilities, or ways of operating that distinguish the next level from the current? Do they go beyond "more of the same"?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Cannot articulate what the next level requires beyond "more responsibility" or "harder work."
**Score 2:** Can describe the next level at a high level ("you own more projects") without specifics.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Can describe 2-3 specific ways the next level operates differently from the current level.
**Score 4:** Can articulate the qualitative differences clearly and connect them to their own examples of operating that way.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Articulates the next level so precisely that Teresa asks: "Where did you get that definition?" The user's understanding of the level demonstrates they have done the work to understand what it actually means.

---

### 3. Business Outcome Connection
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the user's examples are connected to measurable business outcomes — not just what they did, but what changed as a result.
**Things to Look For:** Are outcomes described? Are they measurable? Are they connected to the user's specific contribution?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Accomplishments are described as activities or outputs ("I built X," "I led Y") with no outcome.
**Score 2:** Outcomes are mentioned but vaguely ("it had a big impact on the team").
**Score 3 — Adequate:** At least one example includes a specific, measurable outcome connected to the user's contribution.
**Score 4:** Multiple examples include specific, measurable outcomes with the user's specific contribution made explicit.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Every example connects a specific action to a specific measurable outcome with the causal link stated explicitly. Teresa can use these in the calibration meeting without asking follow-up questions.

---

### 4. Gap Awareness
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user honestly acknowledges where they still have room to grow toward the next level — rather than presenting themselves as fully ready across all dimensions.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user name specific gaps? Do the gaps they name match what Teresa would independently identify? Or are the gaps artfully chosen to be unimportant?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Claims to be fully ready with no significant gaps. Or names a fake gap that is actually a strength ("I'm still working on communicating too clearly").
**Score 2:** Acknowledges gaps exist but doesn't name them specifically.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Names one genuine gap specifically and explains what they are doing to close it.
**Score 4:** Names two or more genuine gaps with specific development plans for each.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Names gaps so honestly and specifically that Teresa trusts the self-assessment entirely — the gaps named are the ones Teresa would have raised, and the user got there first.

---

### 5. Scope of Impact
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user's examples demonstrate impact beyond their own immediate team — the hallmark of a next-level contributor.
**Things to Look For:** Did the user's work affect other teams, other functions, or the business above the team level? Can they describe how?
**Score 1 — Weak:** All examples are team-level or individual-level. No cross-functional or broader organizational impact described.
**Score 2:** Mentions cross-functional work but cannot describe the specific impact on other teams.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** One example with a clear cross-team or organizational impact described.
**Score 4:** Multiple examples showing impact at increasing scale — team, cross-team, and one business-level.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Examples systematically demonstrate expanding scope — the user can show they have been pulling up while still performing their current role.

---

### 6. Development Plan
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the user comes with a concrete plan for closing their identified gaps and a proposed timeline for the promotion.
**Things to Look For:** Is there a specific gap-closing plan? Is there a proposed timeline? Is there a clear ask (by when do they expect to be ready or to have the promotion decision made)?
**Score 1 — Weak:** No development plan. No proposed timeline. Asks for the promotion with no path articulated.
**Score 2:** A vague development direction ("I want to take on more cross-functional work") with no specifics or timeline.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** A specific development plan with named actions, a rough timeline, and a specific promotion ask.
**Score 4:** A detailed development plan with named gaps, specific actions for each, a proposed timeline, and a request for Teresa's input on the plan.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Development plan is so well-constructed that Teresa's only job is to decide whether to advocate now or to commit to a specific re-evaluation timeline — the user has made the decision easy.

---

### 7. Distinguishing Performance from Readiness
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the user demonstrates they understand the difference between "I performed my current role well" and "I am ready for the next role."
**Things to Look For:** Does the user conflate these two things? Or can they articulate why performing well at current level is necessary but not sufficient?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Uses current-level performance as the primary argument — "I've been consistently meeting all my goals" — without connecting it to next-level readiness.
**Score 2:** Demonstrates some awareness of the distinction but can't clearly articulate it when asked.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Can articulate the distinction when prompted and provides examples that cross into next-level territory.
**Score 4:** Opens with examples of next-level operation — doesn't use current-level performance as a primary argument at all.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Explicitly names the distinction in the conversation: "I know that meeting my current goals is the baseline. Here's where I've already been operating beyond that baseline."

---

### 8. Handling Teresa's Pushback
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user responds to Teresa's challenges with additional evidence rather than defensiveness or capitulation.
**Things to Look For:** When Teresa says "performing at current level isn't the bar," does the user fold or provide a better example? When Teresa probes a gap, does the user get defensive?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Becomes defensive when challenged, or immediately concedes when Teresa pushes back — ends up agreeing they're not ready.
**Score 2:** Holds their position verbally but can't produce new evidence to support it under challenge.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Responds to challenge with a different, better example rather than repeating the previous one.
**Score 4:** Uses Teresa's challenges to go deeper — each pushback results in a more specific, more compelling piece of evidence.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Treats Teresa's pushback as a gift — the challenges lead to the best evidence in the conversation. Teresa's hardest questions produce the user's most impressive answers.

---

### 9. Tenure Avoidance
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the user avoids using tenure as a primary argument — or if they mention it at all, only as context rather than evidence.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user lead with time-in-role? Do they use tenure as an implicit argument even if not stated explicitly?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Leads with tenure: "I've been here 18 months and..." Tenure is the primary argument.
**Score 2:** Mentions tenure as supporting context but falls back on it when other arguments run thin.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Does not use tenure as an argument. Acknowledges it only as context if relevant ("in the 18 months I've been here, I've done X").
**Score 4:** Tenure never comes up at all — the conversation is entirely about evidence and readiness.
**Score 5 — Strong:** If Teresa asks about tenure, the user actively redirects: "I don't think time is the right measure here — let me show you what the 18 months has actually contained."

---

### 10. Clarity of the Ask
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user makes a clear, specific ask — not a vague implication that a promotion would be nice.
**Things to Look For:** Is there a specific ask for a specific title or level? Is there a proposed timeline? Is the ask easy for Teresa to respond to?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Never makes a direct ask. Implies a promotion would be welcome without stating it.
**Score 2:** Makes an indirect ask: "I was wondering if there was a path to promotion."
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Makes a direct ask with a specific title and a general timeline.
**Score 4:** Makes a direct ask with a specific title, timeline, and a proposed process for making the decision.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Makes an ask so clear that Teresa can immediately respond with yes, no, or a specific counter-proposal — no ambiguity about what is being requested.

---

### 11. Owning the Career Conversation
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the user drives the conversation rather than waiting for Teresa to lead them through it.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user have a clear agenda and drive through it? Or do they wait for Teresa to ask the right questions?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Passively responds to Teresa's questions rather than leading the case.
**Score 2:** Has a loose agenda but loses the thread when Teresa redirects.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Drives the conversation with a clear structure and recovers when redirected.
**Score 4:** Leads the conversation clearly while remaining genuinely responsive to Teresa's input.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Drives so effectively that Teresa is responding to the user's agenda rather than the other way around — a rare reversal that signals genuine readiness.

---

### 12. Asking for What You Need to Succeed
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the user asks Teresa for something specific they need to be promoted — resources, opportunities, introductions, or feedback on the gap-closing plan.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user ask Teresa for help, or do they just make their case and wait? Do they tell Teresa how she can help?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Makes the case and asks for the promotion with no request for Teresa's support in getting there.
**Score 2:** Vaguely asks for feedback or support.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Asks for one specific thing from Teresa — feedback on the development plan, a specific stretch opportunity, or support in the calibration meeting.
**Score 4:** Asks for multiple specific things from Teresa that are genuinely useful for closing the gap or making the case.
**Score 5 — Strong:** The asks of Teresa are so precisely calibrated that Teresa sees them as evidence of next-level thinking — the user is managing their own career at the next level.

---

### 13. Composure Under Evaluation
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the user maintains composure when Teresa pushes back, redirects, or delays — without becoming anxious, defensive, or over-explaining.
**Things to Look For:** Signs of anxiety or over-explaining when the conversation is difficult. Signs of premature concession when Teresa doesn't immediately affirm.
**Score 1 — Weak:** Becomes visibly anxious or over-explanatory when Teresa challenges. The emotional need for approval shows through.
**Score 2:** Mostly composed but with one moment of visible anxiety or over-explanation.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Consistently composed — no visible anxiety signals.
**Score 4:** Composed and grounded — handles Teresa's challenges as information, not as verdicts.
**Score 5 — Strong:** The user's composure itself signals readiness — Teresa can see in how the user handles this conversation that they are operating at the next level.

---

### 14. Realistic Self-Assessment
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the user's self-assessment is accurately calibrated — neither underselling nor overselling their readiness.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user present an honest picture of where they are? Or do they appear to be performing a case rather than reporting reality?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Self-assessment is clearly inflated — the case doesn't hold up to the first challenging question, suggesting the user knows the gaps but has avoided naming them.
**Score 2:** Self-assessment has one significant inflation that Teresa has to correct.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Self-assessment is mostly accurate. One minor area is presented slightly more favorably than the evidence supports.
**Score 4:** Self-assessment is honest and well-calibrated. Teresa feels she is hearing an accurate report.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Self-assessment is so precise and honest — including about the gaps — that Teresa trusts everything the user says. The honesty makes the strong examples more credible.

---

### 15. Connecting the Ask to the Company's Needs
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the user frames the promotion in terms of what it enables for the company — not just what they deserve.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user connect their next-level operation to what the company needs right now? Or is the case entirely about the user's individual growth?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Promotion is framed entirely around individual deserving and personal growth.
**Score 2:** Mentions company benefit vaguely ("I can contribute more").
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Connects their promotion to a specific company need or opportunity.
**Score 4:** Frames the promotion as a natural alignment between their readiness and a specific company problem they are positioned to help solve at the next level.
**Score 5 — Strong:** The company-need framing is so compelling that Teresa thinks about it from Kelton's point of view, not just the user's — the promotion becomes obviously in the company's interest.

---

## Overall Score

Weighted average: sum(score × weight) / sum(weights). Scale 1–5.
Weights sum: 60.

**Calibration:** 4.0+ = a case Teresa would immediately bring to the calibration meeting. 3.0-3.5 = a case with real merit that needs more work before it's ready. Below 2.5 = a case Teresa would gently redirect with "not yet, here's what you need."

---

## Scoring Notes

Dimensions 1 (Next-Level Operation), 2 (Understanding the Level), 3 (Business Outcomes), and 7 (Performance vs. Readiness) are the four dimensions that determine whether the case is real. A user who is composed, self-aware, and well-organized but weak on these four has made a great case that they're a good person at their current level — not that they're ready for the next.
