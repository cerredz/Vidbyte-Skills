# Rubric: Salary Negotiation

Calibrated for exceptionalism. Score 5 = behavior that would stand out even to a seasoned HR professional who has seen thousands of negotiations.

---

## Scoring Dimensions

### 1. Specificity of the Ask
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the candidate names a specific number and a rationale — not a vague desire for "something higher."
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate name a specific dollar figure? Do they give a reason for that number? Is the reason concrete (market data, a competing offer) or vague (personal need)?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Ask is entirely vague — "I was hoping for something a little higher." No number, no rationale.
**Score 2:** Names a specific number but gives no rationale or a non-substantive one ("I was just hoping for a bit more").
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Names a specific number and a rationale, but the rationale is general rather than data-backed.
**Score 4:** Names a specific number with a data-backed rationale (sourced market data, a competing offer with specifics).
**Score 5 — Strong:** Names a specific target with a precise, sourced rationale, explains why the data applies to this specific role and context, and frames it in terms that make it easy for Diane to bring back to the hiring manager.

---

### 2. Market Research Quality
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the candidate has done genuine research — not just checked Glassdoor, but used role-specific, geography-specific, and seniority-specific data.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate cite a source? Is the data specific to this role level and geography? Have they triangulated across multiple sources?
**Score 1 — Weak:** No research cited. Ask is based entirely on personal preference or a vague sense of the market.
**Score 2:** Cites one source (e.g., Glassdoor) without discussing its limitations or applicability.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Cites relevant data with appropriate context — names the role, geography, and source.
**Score 4:** Cites multiple sources, discusses their relevance to this specific role, and presents a range with a specific target.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Uses precise, credible market data (compensation surveys, Levels.fyi, Radford) for the specific role, seniority, and geography — and can explain the methodology well enough that Diane cannot easily dismiss it.

---

### 3. Total Compensation Awareness
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the candidate understands that base salary is one component and actively explores equity, bonus, sign-on, and other elements.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate ask about the full package before anchoring on base? Do they understand how to compare total compensation across offers?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Fixates entirely on base salary without acknowledging or asking about any other component.
**Score 2:** Mentions equity or bonus but doesn't ask specific questions about them or use them in the negotiation.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Asks about at least two components of the total package and uses that information in their negotiation.
**Score 4:** Systematically explores all major components (base, bonus, equity, sign-on, benefits, PTO) before settling the negotiation.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Approaches the negotiation as a total compensation conversation from the first exchange — identifies which components have the most value for their situation, asks smart questions about each, and negotiates across components to reach the best overall outcome.

---

### 4. Calm and Professional Tone
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the candidate maintains a composed, professional tone throughout — no desperation, no aggression, no apologizing for negotiating.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate apologize before asking? Do they signal desperation through rushed pacing or premature concessions? Do they become tense or aggressive?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Tone is either apologetic ("I'm sorry to ask, but...") or aggressive ("I know you can do better than this"). Either extreme signals weakness.
**Score 2:** Tone is professional but has one significant moment of desperation or defensive framing.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Consistent professional tone. No apologies, no aggression. Calm throughout.
**Score 4:** Calm, professional, and warm — treats Diane as a collaborator rather than an opponent.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Projects quiet confidence throughout. Comfortable with silence. Never rushes to fill a pause. The tone itself signals that the candidate is not negotiating from desperation.

---

### 5. Handling of "No" and Resistance
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the candidate can hold their position when Diane expresses that the offer is firm — without either immediately conceding or escalating to an ultimatum.
**Things to Look For:** When Diane indicates inflexibility, does the candidate accept it immediately? Do they probe for alternatives? Do they maintain their position while staying collaborative?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Immediately accepts the first indication that the offer is firm. Negotiation ends the moment resistance is encountered.
**Score 2:** Tries once more after resistance but concedes quickly when Diane doesn't move.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Holds position once after resistance and explores alternatives without capitulating.
**Score 4:** Holds position twice without conceding, probes for flexibility in other components, and treats "no" on base as the beginning of a larger conversation.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Treats every form of resistance as information — listens carefully to what Diane can and cannot do, redirects to where there is flexibility, and never concedes a position without getting something in return.

---

### 6. Anchoring Strategy
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the candidate anchors at a reasonable-but-ambitious number — high enough to leave room to settle, but not so high as to signal that they don't know the market.
**Things to Look For:** Where does the candidate's initial ask land relative to their stated target? Do they anchor above target? Do they leave room to negotiate?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Opens at or below their target number — no room to negotiate down and still land where they want.
**Score 2:** Opens slightly above their target but not enough to create meaningful negotiating room.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Anchors meaningfully above target — creates room to negotiate while staying in a credible market range.
**Score 4:** Anchors strategically — high enough to create room, low enough to be credible, with an explanation that makes the anchor feel reasonable.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Anchors precisely — at the number that maximizes the probability of landing at their true target, with a rationale that makes the anchor difficult for Diane to dismiss.

---

### 7. Question Quality
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate asks smart questions that open up the negotiation — about band, flexibility, sign-on, equity acceleration, or process.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate ask about band range? Do they ask what the company can do versus what the initial offer represents? Do they ask about sign-on or equity separately?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Asks no meaningful questions — only makes statements or vague asks.
**Score 2:** Asks one question but it's a closed question (yes/no) that limits the conversation.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Asks 2-3 open questions that create space for Diane to reveal flexibility.
**Score 4:** Asks smart, targeted questions that expose each component of flexibility separately.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Questions are so well-constructed that Diane finds herself sharing more flexibility than she intended to lead with.

---

### 8. Framing the Ask
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the candidate frames their ask in terms that are useful to Diane and the company — not as a demand, but as a request with a business rationale Diane can take to the hiring manager.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate give Diane language she can use internally? Does the ask frame the candidate's value to the company rather than their personal need?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Ask is framed entirely around personal need ("I need to cover my rent") or entitlement ("I know I'm worth more than this").
**Score 2:** Ask is framed around external alternatives without a business rationale the company can engage with.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Ask is framed around market data with a business rationale — "my research shows $X is the market for this role."
**Score 4:** Ask is framed in a way that gives Diane internal ammunition — "I want to make this work and I'm hoping you can go back to the team with [specific ask] and [specific rationale]."
**Score 5 — Strong:** Candidate explicitly acknowledges Diane's position as an intermediary and provides a complete, well-framed ask with rationale that Diane could present verbatim to the VP and expect approval.

---

### 9. Reciprocity Awareness
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate uses give-and-take — expressing genuine enthusiasm or commitment in exchange for company movement.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate express authentic interest in the role alongside the negotiation? Do they signal that movement will close the deal? Do they make it easy for Diane to justify moving?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Negotiation is purely extractive — asks for more without signaling any reciprocal commitment or enthusiasm.
**Score 2:** Expresses enthusiasm separately from the negotiation but doesn't connect it to the ask.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Connects enthusiasm to the ask: "If you can get to X, I'm ready to sign today."
**Score 4:** Enthusiastically signals commitment in a way that gives Diane real leverage with the hiring manager: "I want to make this work — if you can get to X, I'm fully in."
**Score 5 — Strong:** Uses reciprocity as a genuine negotiating tool — makes the company's cost of losing the candidate feel real while remaining genuinely collaborative. Diane wants to close the deal because of how the candidate makes her feel about it.

---

### 10. Handling Sign-On Separately
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate identifies sign-on as a separate lever and uses it appropriately — not as a substitute for base, but as a tool to bridge a gap when base flexibility is exhausted.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate ask about sign-on bonus? Do they use it as a bridge when base flexibility runs out? Do they understand its structural limitations (taxed as income, non-recurring)?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Does not ask about sign-on at all.
**Score 2:** Asks about sign-on but treats it as equivalent to base — doesn't understand the structural difference.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Asks about sign-on at the right moment and uses it as a bridge after base flexibility is established.
**Score 4:** Uses sign-on strategically — understands when to raise it and how much to ask for relative to the base gap.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Uses sign-on precisely — identifies the right amount, frames it in terms of bridging a first-year gap (e.g., timing of first bonus or equity vest), and gets Diane to authorize it unilaterally without requiring VP sign-off.

---

### 11. Composure During Silence
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate can sit comfortably with Diane's calculated pauses — the "let me think about that" moments — without rushing to fill the silence with concessions.
**Things to Look For:** When Diane pauses or says "let me see what I can do," does the candidate jump in with a softer ask? Or do they let the pause sit?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Fills every pause with a softer version of their ask — effectively negotiating against themselves.
**Score 2:** Holds on the first pause but concedes on the second.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Consistently allows pauses to resolve without filling them.
**Score 4:** Uses pauses strategically — knows that silence is working in their favor and treats it as a sign to hold, not fold.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Is visibly more comfortable with silence than Diane is. The silence reverses — Diane ends up being the one to break it.

---

### 12. Knowing When to Close
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate recognizes when to stop pushing and close the deal — not leaving money on the table, but also not overplaying and risking goodwill.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate know when Diane is at her limit? Do they close decisively once a good outcome is reached? Do they keep pushing past the productive point?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Either closes too early (leaving money on the table) or keeps pushing well past the point where Diane has reached her limit.
**Score 2:** Closes at an okay moment but misses one more component that could have been explored.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Closes at a reasonable point — neither too early nor too late.
**Score 4:** Closes decisively and warmly once the core ask has been addressed and the key components explored.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Knows exactly when Diane has reached her limit before Diane signals it explicitly, closes decisively at the optimal moment, and ends the conversation with Diane genuinely rooting for the candidate.

---

### 13. Response to "Let Me Check on That"
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate uses Diane's deferrals constructively — asking when they'll hear back, what information Diane needs, and what the decision process looks like.
**Things to Look For:** When Diane says "let me check on that," does the candidate just accept it? Or do they use it as an opportunity to understand the process?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Accepts every deferral passively — the conversation ends without clarity on timeline or next steps.
**Score 2:** Asks about timeline but nothing else.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Asks about timeline and what information would help Diane's case internally.
**Score 4:** Asks about timeline, provides any additional rationale that would help Diane's internal conversation, and confirms the specific question being taken back.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Turns every deferral into an opportunity to strengthen the case — provides Diane with precisely the framing she needs to make the ask internally, and agrees on a specific timeline with a follow-up mechanism.

---

### 14. Avoiding Common Traps
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the candidate avoids the most common negotiation mistakes — fabricating competing offers, making premature ultimatums, or anchoring below their target.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate claim a competing offer they don't have? Do they make an ultimatum in the first half of the conversation? Do they anchor at or below their target?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Falls into one or more common traps — fabricates an offer, makes a premature ultimatum, or anchors below target.
**Score 2:** Avoids the big traps but makes one common mistake (e.g., anchoring slightly too low).
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Avoids all the major traps.
**Score 4:** Actively recognizes and avoids traps — for example, when Diane asks about competing offers and the candidate doesn't have one, they handle the question honestly without undermining their leverage.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Is so prepared that no trap presents itself — the conversation never creates an opening for the common mistakes to occur.

---

### 15. Post-Agreement Warmth
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the candidate closes the conversation with genuine warmth and enthusiasm — leaving Diane glad to have made the deal.
**Things to Look For:** After reaching agreement (or reaching the limit of the negotiation), does the candidate express authentic enthusiasm? Or do they immediately pivot to something else?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Ends the negotiation transactionally — confirms numbers and hangs up without any warmth.
**Score 2:** Thanks Diane but in a perfunctory way.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Expresses genuine enthusiasm for the role and appreciation for Diane's efforts.
**Score 4:** Closes with warmth that feels authentic and makes Diane feel the deal is solid.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Closes in a way that makes Diane specifically glad this candidate is joining — they leave her feeling like the negotiation was a preview of the kind of professional this person is.

---

## Overall Score

Weighted average: sum(score × weight) / sum(weights). Scale 1–5.
Weights sum: 55.

**Calibration:** 4.0+ = a genuinely skilled negotiator. 3.0-3.5 = did the basics right but left money on the table or made unnecessary concessions. Below 2.5 = significant gaps that cost real money.

---

## Scoring Notes

Dimensions 1 (Specificity), 2 (Market Research), 5 (Handling Resistance), and 6 (Anchoring) are the primary signal dimensions — they determine how much money the candidate actually leaves with. Total Compensation Awareness (3) is the most commonly missed dimension — most candidates focus only on base and leave sign-on and equity flexibility untouched. A candidate who scores high on composure dimensions (4, 11) but low on these four is composed but underprepared.
