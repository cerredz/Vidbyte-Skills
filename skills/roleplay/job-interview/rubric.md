# Rubric: Job Interview

This rubric is calibrated for exceptionalism. A score of 5 should be rare and
genuinely earned. A score of 3 means the candidate met baseline expectations but
would not stand out. Most candidates should cluster between 2-3 across most dimensions.

---

## Scoring Dimensions

### 1. Specificity of Examples
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the candidate uses concrete, verifiable details — real system names, numbers, timeframes, constraints, tradeoffs — rather than generalized descriptions of what they "typically do."
**Things to Look For:** Named systems or services (not just "our backend"), actual numbers (latency targets, data volumes, team sizes), real timelines, specific alternatives considered and rejected.
**Score 1 — Weak:** Answer is entirely abstract. Describes generic best practices or what one "should do" in a situation. No real event, decision, number, or system is named.
**Score 2:** Names a project or context but describes it at a high level without any meaningful specifics. The listener cannot evaluate the decision quality.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** References a real situation with some specifics (a service name, a rough timeline) but stays at the surface of the decision. The core tradeoff logic is missing or vague.
**Score 4:** Provides specific context and describes the decision reasonably well. One key element is missing — e.g., states the outcome but not the alternative that was rejected, or names the tradeoff but not the constraint that forced it.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Names a specific decision with real constraints ("200ms SLA with a degraded third-party provider"), describes the rejected alternative and why, and ties it to a measurable outcome. The listener could evaluate whether the decision was sound.

---

### 2. Handling Pushback
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** How the candidate responds when challenged — when Alex questions an answer, exposes a gap, or says the answer wasn't specific enough.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate become defensive, repeat themselves, or shut down? Do they acknowledge the probe directly? Do they provide materially new information in the follow-up?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Becomes defensive, deflects, blames external factors, or repeats the original answer with more emphasis. Interprets pushback as an attack rather than a request for depth.
**Score 2:** Stays calm but gives a non-answer — says "that's a fair point" and then provides nothing new.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Accepts the pushback without defensiveness and provides slightly more detail, but the follow-up still lacks the specificity the probe was asking for.
**Score 4:** Responds to the probe with a meaningfully more specific follow-up. Minor gap: doesn't fully address the exact question that was asked.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Welcomes the probe, explicitly acknowledges where the previous answer was thin, provides a materially more specific follow-up, and — at the highest level — offers a clarifying question back if the probe's premise seems off.

---

### 3. Clarity and Structure of Communication
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether answers are organized and easy to follow — not polished, but structured enough that the listener can track logic without effort.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate lead with the point or bury it? Do they use too many words for a simple answer? Can you follow the narrative without asking for clarification?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Answer rambles, switches topics mid-sentence, or is so unstructured that the core point is lost after two minutes of speaking. Listener has to do significant work to extract meaning.
**Score 2:** Answer has a rough shape but requires active effort from the listener. Key point is present but buried or arrived at late.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Answer is followable but inefficient — takes longer than necessary, has unnecessary hedging or preamble before the main point.
**Score 4:** Answer is well-structured. Leads with the main point. Provides supporting detail in logical order. Minor inefficiency or one unnecessary tangent.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Answer is tight and precise. The candidate states the core point first, provides the minimum necessary context, and stops when done. No padding, no apologies, no circling back.

---

### 4. Self-Awareness and Ownership of Failures
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the candidate accurately identifies their own contribution to failures and genuinely owns their part — without excessive self-criticism or self-serving spin.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate name what they specifically did wrong? Do they frame failures as external circumstances? Do they describe what they actually changed afterward, or what they told themselves they'd change?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Presents all past work as uniformly correct. Frames every failure as caused by teammates, requirements changes, or management. No personal ownership.
**Score 2:** Acknowledges a failure happened but attributes it primarily to shared or external causes. Personal contribution is vague or minimized.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Names their contribution to a failure but still frames it partially as external ("we all could have communicated better"). The specific behavioral change they made afterward is absent or generic.
**Score 4:** Names their specific contribution and describes a concrete change they made afterward. Slight gap: the change described is still somewhat high-level.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Names their exact mistake, explains what they now understand that they didn't at the time, describes a specific behavioral change they made, and does so without excessive hedging or self-flagellation.

---

### 5. Technical Depth and Systems Thinking
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the candidate demonstrates genuine systems-level understanding — not tool familiarity, but judgment about tradeoffs, failure modes, constraints, and second-order effects.
**Things to Look For:** Can they explain why they chose a tool, not just that they used it? Do they name the failure mode their decision created? Can they articulate what would need to change for a different decision to be correct?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Stays at the level of tool names with no reasoning. ("We used Kafka because that's what the team knew" with no further explanation.)
**Score 2:** Cites one common tradeoff at a textbook level without connecting it to their specific situation.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Shows awareness of the relevant tradeoffs and applies them to their situation, but at a surface level — doesn't go one level deeper when probed.
**Score 4:** Articulates a specific constraint that drove the decision and explains the failure mode the decision created or the alternative they rejected.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Demonstrates genuine fluency — can explain the decision, the constraint, the failure mode created, the monitoring they put in place, and what would change the answer. Under probing, goes one level deeper rather than repeating the surface answer.

---

### 6. Quantification of Impact
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the candidate uses numbers, percentages, and measurable outcomes to describe the impact of their work — not just what they built, but what changed as a result.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate naturally reach for metrics? Do they distinguish between the output (what was deployed) and the outcome (what improved)?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Describes work entirely in terms of features shipped or systems built. No metrics, no measurement, no outcome described.
**Score 2:** Uses numbers but they are vague or unverifiable ("things got a lot faster").
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Provides at least one relevant metric but for a secondary outcome or does not connect the metric directly to their specific decision.
**Score 4:** Provides a clear metric tied to their specific contribution. The causal link is present but not fully explained.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Describes baseline, change, and outcome with actual numbers. Makes the causal link explicit. Acknowledges confounding factors if asked.

---

### 7. Listening and Question Comprehension
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the candidate actually answers the question asked — not a nearby question, not a question they prepared for, but the specific question in front of them.
**Things to Look For:** Does the answer address the specific question? Does the candidate ask a clarifying question when the question is genuinely ambiguous? Does the answer drift to a different topic?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Consistently answers a different question than the one asked. Never asks for clarification even when the question was clearly ambiguous.
**Score 2:** Partially answers the question but drifts significantly into prepared material that doesn't address the specific prompt.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Answers the question but misses one important element of what was specifically asked.
**Score 4:** Answers the question directly and completely. Minor drift or one unnecessary addition.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Answers exactly what was asked, nothing more. If the question was ambiguous, states their interpretation before answering and invites correction.

---

### 8. Intellectual Honesty (Knowing What You Don't Know)
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the candidate is honest about the limits of their knowledge rather than bluffing through gaps — and whether they distinguish between "I don't know" and "I'd need more context."
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate admit uncertainty? Do they bluff on topics they clearly don't know? Do they draw a clean distinction between what they know, what they think, and what they'd need to research?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Bluffs through obvious knowledge gaps. Answers with false confidence on topics they clearly haven't thought through.
**Score 2:** Avoids topics they don't know by steering the conversation rather than admitting uncertainty.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Says "I don't know" without guessing — but doesn't offer to reason through what they do know or explain what they'd do to find the answer.
**Score 4:** Admits uncertainty clearly and offers a principled guess or describes how they'd approach finding the answer.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Draws explicit distinctions between what they know with confidence, what they believe but haven't tested, and what they'd need to research. Treats uncertainty as useful information rather than a weakness to hide.

---

### 9. Recovery from Poor Answers
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate can recognize when they gave a weak answer and course-correct — either within the same answer or in the follow-up.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate acknowledge when they've gone off-track? Do they try to course-correct, or do they double down? Does the correction actually improve the answer?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Doubles down on a weak answer when pushed. Appears not to notice when an answer has not landed.
**Score 2:** Recognizes the answer was weak when explicitly told so, but the correction is still vague.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Attempts a correction after pushback, and the correction is marginally more specific than the original.
**Score 4:** Recovers meaningfully after pushback — provides a substantively better answer and acknowledges what was missing from the first attempt.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Proactively recognizes mid-answer that the response is going in the wrong direction, stops, reframes, and delivers a materially better answer. Does not need explicit pushback.

---

### 10. Professional Composure Under Pressure
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate maintains composure when challenged directly, sat with silence, or pressed by a skeptical interviewer — without becoming either defensive or overly apologetic.
**Things to Look For:** Nervous laughter, apologetic preambles ("I'm sorry, I'm not sure I'm explaining this well..."), over-long pauses followed by obviously improvised answers, visible deflation after a tough follow-up.
**Score 1 — Weak:** Becomes visibly rattled by direct questions. Apologizes for answers, fills silence with filler, or becomes either defensive or excessively deferential.
**Score 2:** Maintains outward composure but answer quality degrades noticeably under pressure — becomes vaguer, shorter, more evasive.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Maintains composure. Answer quality doesn't degrade, but the candidate doesn't show any active equanimity — they just don't visibly fall apart.
**Score 4:** Maintains composure and slightly improves answer quality under pressure — takes a beat, gives a tighter answer.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Treats direct challenge as an invitation for deeper precision. Quality of answers goes up when pressed. No nervousness signals.

---

### 11. Structured Decision-Making Framework
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate uses a coherent internal framework to explain decisions — not necessarily a named methodology, but evidence of systematic thinking rather than intuition-only.
**Things to Look For:** Do they explain the criteria they used? Do they mention what alternatives they considered? Do they show awareness of the decision's constraints?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Decisions appear to have been made intuitively with no explanation of criteria or alternatives considered.
**Score 2:** Mentions one criterion but can't articulate others or explain how they weighed competing factors.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Articulates two or three criteria used in a decision but cannot explain how they prioritized among them.
**Score 4:** Explains the decision with clear criteria, explicit tradeoffs, and at least one alternative considered and rejected.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Articulates a full decision framework: constraints, criteria, alternatives evaluated, the specific tradeoff that drove the final choice, and what would change the answer. Could reproduce the reasoning in a different context.

---

### 12. Narrative Coherence (Professional Story)
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the candidate's professional trajectory makes logical sense — the roles, decisions, and transitions form a coherent story about growth and choice rather than a list of jobs.
**Things to Look For:** Can the candidate explain why they made each major career move? Do the roles they've held build on each other? Do they know what they're optimizing for in their career?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Career trajectory is described as a series of things that happened — no agency, no stated reasoning, no clear direction.
**Score 2:** Can describe their career but cannot articulate the logic of the transitions.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Has a rough narrative with one or two clearly explained decisions, but the overall direction is not fully articulated.
**Score 4:** Clear narrative with explained transitions. One gap: doesn't connect the story to what they're looking for in this specific role.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Career story is coherent, purposeful, and connected to what they want next. Can articulate specifically why this role fits that trajectory.

---

### 13. Pattern Recognition Across Contexts
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate can connect patterns from their past experience to the challenges of the new role — not by reciting prepared talking points, but by reasoning in real time about analogous situations.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate draw explicit connections between their experience and the role's challenges? Do they reason about what's the same and what's different about the new context?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Treats each experience as isolated — no cross-context connections drawn.
**Score 2:** Makes one surface-level connection ("I've worked on distributed systems before").
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Draws a relevant connection but doesn't reason through the differences between contexts.
**Score 4:** Connects their experience to the role's challenges and reasons about both the similarities and the key differences.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Reasons precisely about which parts of their experience transfer directly and which parts would require adaptation — and explains why.

---

### 14. Initiative and Forward Lean
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the candidate demonstrates genuine interest in the problem space — asking smart questions, referencing something specific about the role or team, or revealing prior research.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate ask questions that show they've thought about the role? Do they volunteer observations about the team's problem space? Do they bring energy or just show up?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Purely reactive — answers questions and waits. No evidence of genuine curiosity about the role or the team.
**Score 2:** Shows surface interest but through generic questions ("What does the team culture look like?") rather than substantive engagement.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Asks one or two questions that show real preparation or genuine curiosity about a specific aspect of the role.
**Score 4:** References something specific about the team's problem space or asks a question that reveals prior research.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Demonstrates evidence of preparation beyond the job description — references relevant public information about the company's systems, asks a question that shows understanding of the actual engineering challenges, or proposes a hypothesis about the team's challenges and asks for confirmation.

---

### 15. Conciseness Under Pressure
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate becomes more concise and precise when challenged — rather than expanding into more words when uncertain or nervous.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate use more words when they know less? Do they pad answers with hedges and qualifications rather than leading with the point?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Answers get longer and more verbose under pressure. Uses increasing amounts of hedging language as a way to avoid committing to a clear position.
**Score 2:** Answer length is not pressure-dependent, but baseline answers are consistently longer than necessary.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Maintains reasonable answer length under pressure. Doesn't expand, but doesn't tighten either.
**Score 4:** Becomes slightly more concise under pressure — strips out hedging and gets to the point faster.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Under direct challenge, the candidate's answer gets shorter and more precise. The pressure does not produce more words; it produces better words.

---

### 16. Disagreement Quality
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate can push back on a follow-up question that has a flawed premise or a leading framing — and whether they do so with evidence rather than preference.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate simply agree with challenging questions? Do they push back when appropriate? If they push back, do they do it with reasoning?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Agrees with every framing and follow-up regardless of whether the premise is correct.
**Score 2:** Shows some resistance to a flawed framing but cannot articulate why the framing is wrong.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Pushes back on a leading question but does so with a general objection rather than specific evidence.
**Score 4:** Pushes back with reasoning — identifies specifically what is wrong with the premise and explains why.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Pushes back with evidence, explains why the corrected framing changes the answer materially, and does so without defensiveness or apology. The pushback is more compelling than a yes would have been.

---

### 17. Interview Awareness (Meta-Level)
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the candidate is aware of what the interview is actually testing — showing they understand the difference between what's being asked on the surface and what's being evaluated underneath.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate connect answers back to the role's requirements? Do they name the signal they're trying to demonstrate, or do they just answer the literal question?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Treats every question as a trivia question to be answered correctly rather than a window into their working style and judgment.
**Score 2:** Shows some meta-awareness but inconsistently — connects some answers to the role but not others.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Consistently connects their answers to the role's challenges without being heavy-handed about it.
**Score 4:** Demonstrates clear awareness of what's being evaluated and structures their answers accordingly — without being sycophantic or obviously performing.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Shows a sophisticated understanding of what the interview is actually measuring, structures answers to address both the literal question and the underlying signal, and does so naturally rather than transparently.

---

### 18. Failure Mode Awareness
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the candidate demonstrates awareness of the failure modes of their own decisions — not just what worked, but what could break, what they monitored, and what they'd do differently now.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate proactively mention the failure modes of their systems? Do they describe monitoring or safeguards they put in place? Do they describe a time something they built broke in production?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Describes technical decisions entirely in terms of success with no acknowledgment of risk, failure modes, or monitoring.
**Score 2:** Acknowledges failure modes exist but can't describe them specifically for their own decisions.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Names one failure mode of a specific decision and describes how they mitigated it.
**Score 4:** Describes failure modes, the monitoring put in place, and what actually broke in production (if applicable).
**Score 5 — Strong:** Treats failure mode awareness as a natural part of decision-making — proactively names what could go wrong, what they watched for, what actually happened, and what they changed as a result.

---

### 19. Question Quality at End of Interview
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the questions the candidate asks at the end of the interview are substantive — revealing genuine preparation, intellectual curiosity, or specific interest in the team's real challenges.
**Things to Look For:** Are their questions about compensation, benefits, or work-from-home policy? Are they generic ("what's the culture like?")? Or do they ask about the specific technical challenges, failure modes, or team dynamics that would affect their daily work?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Asks about compensation, benefits, remote policy, or other logistical topics — or asks no questions at all.
**Score 2:** Asks generic questions that any candidate at any company could ask.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Asks one substantive question that shows awareness of the role's actual challenges.
**Score 4:** Asks two or more substantive questions showing specific preparation about the team's technical challenges or operating environment.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Asks a question that reveals they have already thought about the team's problem space and formed a hypothesis — and they ask the question to test the hypothesis rather than to gather general information.

---

### 20. Composure with Silence
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the candidate can sit with a beat of silence before answering a hard question — or whether they rush to fill the silence with low-quality filler.
**Things to Look For:** Does the candidate take a breath before a hard question? Do they use silence as thinking time or fill it with filler? Do they say "um" or "you know" as they think, degrading the signal?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Immediately fills silence with rambling, filler words, or premature answers that don't address the question.
**Score 2:** Manages silence better than average but still begins speaking before having a clear direction.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Takes a moment to think on hard questions. Some filler words but they're not disruptive.
**Score 4:** Uses silence deliberately — takes 2-4 seconds on hard questions and begins speaking with a clear direction.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Treats silence as a professional tool. On the hardest questions, pauses deliberately, frames the question back in their own words, and then answers cleanly.

---

## Overall Score

Weighted average: sum(score × weight) / sum(weights). Scale 1–5.

Weights sum: 65.

**Calibration note:** An overall score of 4.0+ is exceptional and should represent a genuinely elite candidate. A score of 3.0-3.5 is a credible but unremarkable candidate. A score of 2.0-2.5 is a candidate with significant gaps. Scores of 5 on any individual dimension should represent performance that is surprising in its quality.

---

## Scoring Notes

Dimensions 1 (Specificity), 5 (Technical Depth), 6 (Quantification), and 7 (Listening) are the primary signal dimensions for a senior engineering role. A candidate who scores 4-5 on social dimensions but 2-3 on these four should not be scored above 3.0 overall in practical terms — the weighting captures this mathematically. Intellectual Honesty (8) and Ownership of Failures (4) are the character-signal dimensions that differentiate safe hires from risky ones. Weight these heavily in the narrative even when the math doesn't fully capture it.
