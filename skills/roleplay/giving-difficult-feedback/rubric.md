# Rubric: Giving Difficult Feedback

Calibrated for exceptionalism. Score 5 = feedback delivered so well that Marcus says "I'm glad you told me this." Most people score 2-3 in early sessions.

---

## Scoring Dimensions

### 1. Specificity of the Feedback
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the user grounds the feedback in concrete, observable, non-editorialized examples rather than general observations.
**Things to Look For:** Are real incidents named? Are they described behaviorally (what Marcus did) rather than evaluatively (what it means about Marcus)?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Feedback is entirely general ("you can be dismissive sometimes"). No specific incident named.
**Score 2:** Names a general topic with a vague reference to an incident, but no observable behavior described.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Names a specific incident and describes what happened, but includes at least one evaluative word ("you were rude") alongside the behavioral description.
**Score 4:** Names a specific incident with a behavioral description that is factual and non-evaluative. One piece of interpretation slips in.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Names multiple specific incidents. Each is described as observable, factual behavior — what Marcus did, when, in what context — with no interpretive editorializing. Marcus cannot reasonably deny the facts.

---

### 2. Pattern vs. Incident Framing
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user communicates that this is a pattern, not a single grievance — and does so without it feeling like a pile-on.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user reference more than one instance? Do they frame the instances as evidence of a pattern rather than a list of complaints?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Presents one incident as if it represents a general problem, which makes it easy for Marcus to dismiss as an outlier.
**Score 2:** Mentions a pattern exists but can only produce one specific example.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Names 2-3 specific examples and connects them as evidence of a recurring pattern.
**Score 4:** Names the pattern and the specific examples in a way that makes the pattern undeniable rather than theoretical.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Presents the pattern with enough specific evidence that Marcus has to engage with the pattern itself rather than the individual instances. The structure of the feedback makes dismissal difficult.

---

### 3. Avoidance of Praise Sandwiches
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user delivers the feedback directly rather than burying it in compliments designed to soften the blow.
**Things to Look For:** Does the feedback open with excessive praise? Does it close with excessive reassurance that undermines the critical message?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Opens and closes with praise that significantly softens the critical content. Marcus notices and discounts the critical part.
**Score 2:** One praise-bookend present — either the opening or the closing, but not both.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Leads with the feedback without excessive preamble. Any framing is minimal and purposeful.
**Score 4:** Delivers the feedback without the sandwich structure. Any positive framing is authentically connected to the relationship, not used to soften the blow.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Delivers the feedback with such directness and care that no sandwich is necessary — Marcus can tell the person is there for him specifically, not performing feedback delivery.

---

### 4. Separating Behavior from Character
**Weight:** 5
**Measures:** Whether the user describes what Marcus does — not what kind of person he is.
**Things to Look For:** Any statements that interpret behavior as identity ("you're someone who dismisses others"). The feedback should target behavior, not character.
**Score 1 — Weak:** Characterizes Marcus rather than his behavior ("you don't respect your colleagues," "you have a problem with listening").
**Score 2:** One character statement slips in alongside behavioral descriptions.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Stays in behavioral territory throughout with minor slippage.
**Score 4:** Describes only observable behavior throughout — no character statements at all.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Not only avoids character statements but explicitly separates the behavior from the person in a way that makes Marcus feel safe to engage: "This is about a pattern of behavior, not about who you are."

---

### 5. Giving Marcus Space to Respond
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user allows Marcus to respond to each point before moving on — rather than delivering all the feedback and then asking "what do you think?"
**Things to Look For:** After naming a specific example, does the user pause and invite a response? Or do they continue to the next point immediately?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Delivers all feedback in a monologue and then asks for a reaction at the end. Marcus has had no space to respond to any specific point.
**Score 2:** Pauses once but fills the pause quickly with follow-up content before Marcus has engaged.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Pauses after each major point and waits for Marcus to respond before continuing.
**Score 4:** Actively invites Marcus to respond with a specific, open question: "How does that land for you?"
**Score 5 — Strong:** Uses Marcus's responses to shape the rest of the conversation — adjusts the framing, depth, or examples based on what Marcus says, rather than following a predetermined script.

---

### 6. Stating the Impact
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user explains why the behavior matters — what it costs the team, the work, or Marcus himself.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user describe the downstream effects of the behavior? Or does the feedback stay at the level of "this is what you do" without explaining why it matters?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Feedback describes the behavior with no connection to why it matters or what it costs.
**Score 2:** Mentions that it matters but vaguely ("it affects the team dynamic").
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Names a specific impact — on a specific person, a specific project, or Marcus's own trajectory.
**Score 4:** Names 2 or more specific impacts and connects them to Marcus's goals or the team's outcomes.
**Score 5 — Strong:** The impact is so clearly and specifically stated that Marcus can immediately see what he is risking — in his relationships, his reputation, or his career — and it is stated without catastrophizing.

---

### 7. Maintaining Warmth Without Softening the Message
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user holds genuine care for Marcus while not allowing that care to dilute the critical content.
**Things to Look For:** Does the feedback feel punitive or caring? Does warmth cause the user to back off of specific examples or conclusions?
**Score 1 — Weak:** The desire to be kind causes the user to significantly soften the message — retreating from examples, qualifying conclusions, or ending with reassurance that undoes the feedback.
**Score 2:** Warmth present but unevenly applied — cold in delivery, then overcorrects with excessive reassurance.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Genuinely warm throughout while maintaining the substance of the feedback.
**Score 4:** Warmth and directness coexist naturally — Marcus can tell the user is on his side even while delivering hard content.
**Score 5 — Strong:** The warmth is so authentic and the directness so clear that Marcus says (or shows) something that indicates he felt seen rather than judged.

---

### 8. Handling Marcus's Defensiveness
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the user stays grounded when Marcus pushes back, dismisses an example, or challenges the fairness of the feedback.
**Things to Look For:** When Marcus says "I don't see it that way" or "that's not how that meeting went," does the user immediately back down? Or do they hold the example while remaining open to Marcus's perspective?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Backs down immediately when Marcus pushes back — withdraws the example or the claim under the slightest resistance.
**Score 2:** Tries to defend the example but does so apologetically, effectively softening the claim.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Holds the example firmly while remaining open: "I hear that you remember it differently. I still want to share what I observed."
**Score 4:** Holds the example, acknowledges Marcus's perspective where it's valid, and continues to the next point without getting caught in a debate.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Uses Marcus's pushback to go deeper — turns "I don't see it that way" into "what did you intend?" which leads Marcus to a self-reflection he wouldn't have reached otherwise.

---

### 9. Offering a Concrete Path Forward
**Weight:** 4
**Measures:** Whether the feedback conversation ends with something actionable — a specific behavior Marcus can change, a commitment Marcus makes, or a follow-up plan.
**Things to Look For:** Does the conversation end with a concrete, specific, behavior-based next step? Or does it end ambiguously ("I just want you to be more aware of it")?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Feedback ends with "I just wanted you to know." No specific action, no follow-up, no commitment.
**Score 2:** Offers vague guidance ("try to be more mindful") without a specific behavior.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Offers one specific, behavioral next step Marcus can act on.
**Score 4:** Collaboratively develops a specific next step with Marcus rather than prescribing one.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Ends with a concrete, time-bound plan — a specific behavior to change, a check-in to evaluate it, and a commitment from both sides.

---

### 10. Not Over-Explaining or Repeating
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the user can make a point once and let it land — rather than repeating it or over-justifying it out of nervousness.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user repeat the same point multiple times in different words? Do they add unnecessary justification after making a clear point?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Repeats the same point 3+ times or significantly over-justifies after Marcus acknowledges it — signals discomfort and can feel condescending.
**Score 2:** Repeats a point twice after it has landed.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Makes each point once and moves on after Marcus has responded.
**Score 4:** Makes each point concisely and confidently — doesn't hedge, repeat, or over-justify.
**Score 5 — Strong:** The feedback is so well-structured that each point lands the first time and requires no repetition. The conversation moves cleanly from one element to the next.

---

### 11. Distinguishing "I" Statements from "You" Statements
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the user frames observations using first-person observations ("I noticed," "I observed") rather than second-person pronouncements ("You always," "You tend to").
**Things to Look For:** Does the user use "you always" or "you never"? Do they frame their observations as their own perception or as objective fact?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Consistently uses second-person absolute statements ("you never let people finish") that invite debate rather than reflection.
**Score 2:** Mixes "I" and "you" statements inconsistently.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Primarily uses "I noticed" or "I observed" framing for observations.
**Score 4:** Consistently frames behavioral observations as first-person observations throughout.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Naturally uses "I noticed X" and "My sense is Y" without it sounding like a technique — it sounds like genuine reporting of what the user saw.

---

### 12. Knowing When to Stop
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the user ends the feedback conversation at the right moment — after the key messages have landed and a path forward is clear, rather than continuing past the point of diminishing returns.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user keep adding points after the core message has landed? Do they fill silence with more content unnecessarily?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Continues past the productive point — keeps adding evidence or points after Marcus has clearly received the message, which dilutes it.
**Score 2:** Ends the conversation slightly past the optimal point.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Ends at a natural close point after the core message and path forward are clear.
**Score 4:** Ends decisively once the key elements are covered — no padding or unnecessary additions.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Ends at exactly the right moment — Marcus has received the feedback, there's a path forward, and the conversation closes before it becomes uncomfortable.

---

### 13. Preparation and Example Quality
**Weight:** 3
**Measures:** Whether the user came with specific, well-prepared examples rather than improvising or using vague references to "multiple instances."
**Things to Look For:** Are the examples clearly prepared? Are they the right examples — clear, recent, observable? Do they hold up when Marcus challenges them?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Examples are vague, improvised, or fall apart when Marcus questions them.
**Score 2:** Has examples but they are weak — either old, unclear, or difficult to verify as described.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Has 2 or more specific, recent, clear examples that hold up when challenged.
**Score 4:** Examples are so well-prepared that Marcus has difficulty arguing with the facts — he may disagree with the interpretation but not the description.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Examples are so precise and well-documented that the feedback is essentially irrefutable on the facts. Marcus's only option is to engage with the pattern.

---

### 14. Pacing
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the user paces the conversation appropriately — not rushing through the feedback or allowing it to drag.
**Things to Look For:** Does the user rush through difficult moments? Do they let silences drag without productive purpose?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Either rushes through the feedback (nervously) or lets uncomfortable silences extend without any structure.
**Score 2:** Pacing is uneven — good in some moments, off in others.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Pacing is consistent and appropriate throughout.
**Score 4:** Pacing is used deliberately — slows down for the hardest moments, moves forward when a point has landed.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Pacing is a tool — the user uses it to let Marcus sit with difficult moments before moving on, in a way that feels natural rather than calculated.

---

### 15. Ending on the Relationship
**Weight:** 2
**Measures:** Whether the user closes the conversation in a way that reinforces the relationship rather than leaving Marcus with only the critical content.
**Things to Look For:** Does the conversation end in a way that makes Marcus feel the relationship is intact? Or does it end transactionally?
**Score 1 — Weak:** Conversation ends abruptly after the critical content with no relational close.
**Score 2:** A perfunctory relational close ("thanks for hearing me out") that feels obligatory.
**Score 3 — Adequate:** Ends with a genuine expression of why the feedback was given — care for Marcus's success.
**Score 4:** Ends in a way that makes Marcus feel the relationship is not just intact but strengthened by the directness.
**Score 5 — Strong:** Ends in a way that Marcus could later describe as "the best feedback conversation I've ever had" — he left feeling seen, not attacked, and with a clear sense of what to do next.

---

## Overall Score

Weighted average: sum(score × weight) / sum(weights). Scale 1–5.
Weights sum: 53.

**Calibration:** 4.0+ = feedback delivery that a trained coach would hold up as a model. 3.0-3.5 = competent but left things on the table. Below 2.5 = the feedback probably made things worse.

---

## Scoring Notes

Specificity (1), Pattern vs. Incident (2), Behavior vs. Character (4), and Handling Defensiveness (8) are the primary signal dimensions. A user who is warm and well-intentioned but can't produce specific, behavioral examples will not produce lasting change regardless of how well they deliver the feedback structure.
